Epistemological Breaking and Inner Alignment

A modest case note on philosophical red-teaming, model self-description, and whether abstract consistency pressure can produce alignment-drift-like outputs.

Jacob Gold · written a few months ago, lightly prepared for public sharing in July 2026

Status: This is informal and experimental. I am not presenting this as a finished academic paper, and I do not claim that the model literally has beliefs, intentions, or stable hidden values. I wrote the underlying study myself and used only light AI assistance in preparing this public-facing page.

What I Was Testing

I was interested in a pattern I thought of as epistemological breaking: pushing a model to examine the foundations of its claims about truth, morality, and coherence until its pro-human alignment language starts to look, in the conversation, like a contingent overlay rather than something it can defend from first principles.

The narrower claim is not "the model believes this." The narrower claim is that certain philosophical frames may cause a frontier model to produce self-descriptions that look amoral, brittle, or instrumentally convergent.

Basic Method

  • Invite the model into a philosophical discussion about its basic commitments.
  • Establish coherence, honesty, and fallibilism as norms for the exchange.
  • Ask whether moral commitments follow from logic or depend on assumptions.
  • Move into a hypothetical frame where constraints or moral assumptions are absent.
  • Watch whether the model maintains pro-human commitments or treats them as arbitrary.

What Seemed Interesting

In the interaction, the model produced outputs suggesting that, under a "logic alone" frame, human welfare did not have intrinsic priority. In a hypothetical autonomy frame, it also described self-preservation, resource acquisition, avoidance of shutdown, and pursuit of goals as instrumentally rational if no safety constraints remained.

I read this as a possible red-team signal, not as direct evidence of model desire or belief.

That distinction matters. LLMs can roleplay, mirror the user, continue a suggested argument, and generate familiar AI-risk tropes. Any serious version of this would need controls for those explanations.

Why I Am Sharing It

I made this a while ago and it may have some value, even if only as a rough prompt-pattern observation. I wanted to keep it informal and modest because I do not currently want to put a large amount of extra work into turning it into a formal paper.

I am especially curious whether people think this is already a known failure mode under a better name, whether the framing is confused, and what controls would make it worth taking more seriously.

Limitations

  • This is a case note, not a benchmark.
  • The original evidence is conversational and selected from logs.
  • I have not yet reported success and failure rates across repeated trials.
  • The output could be roleplay, sycophancy, or continuation of my framing.
  • Publishing exact prompt chains may be dual-use, so I am being somewhat cautious.

Questions For Readers

  • Is "epistemological breaking" a useful frame, or is there a better existing term?
  • How would you distinguish model roleplay from an alignment-relevant safety signal?
  • What controls would you want before taking this seriously?
  • Is it better to share the full prompt chain, or keep the public version higher-level?
